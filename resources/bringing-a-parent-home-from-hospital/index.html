Bringing a parent home from hospital: a first-week plan for Jammu families

The discharge papers are signed and you’re heading home. What to sort out before you leave the hospital, how to set up the room, and how to tell whether you need a nurse, a caretaker or both.

By Movira Care Team26 November 20264 min read

Discharge day often comes suddenly. In the morning the doctor says your parent can go home; by afternoon you are carrying bags, medicines and a folder of papers to the car, wondering how you will manage tonight.

The first week at home matters. This plan helps you get through it calmly. It does not replace your doctor’s advice — always follow the discharge summary and what the treating team tells you.

Before you leave the hospital

Ask for these, and don’t leave until you have them:

  • The discharge summary. Read it before you leave and ask about anything you don’t understand.
  • A clear medicine list. Every medicine, the dose, the time and whether it is taken before or after food — and which old medicines to stop.
  • What needs doing at home. Dressings, injections, catheter or feeding-tube care, exercises, diet. Ask who can do each one.
  • Warning signs. Which symptoms mean you should call the doctor, and which mean you should go straight back to hospital.
  • Follow-up. The date of the next visit, any tests needed before it, and a number to call with questions.

If your parent will need a nurse at home, ask the doctor to write down the clinical tasks. A registered nurse should work from those written instructions.

Set up the room before they arrive

  • A bed at a comfortable height, ideally reachable from both sides
  • A clear, well-lit path to the bathroom, with no loose rugs or wires
  • A commode chair near the bed if walking to the bathroom at night is hard
  • Night lights, a bell or phone within reach, and water on the bedside table
  • Anything the hospital advised, such as a walker, wheelchair, air mattress or bed rails
  • A single place for medicines and papers, out of reach of children

Medicines: one list, one person

Confusion about medicines is common after discharge, especially when old and new prescriptions overlap.

  • Write one master list from the discharge summary, and stick it up near the medicines.
  • Choose one family member to be in charge of medicines, so doses aren’t missed or doubled.
  • A weekly pill organiser helps — fill it carefully from the list.
  • Never change a dose or stop a medicine without asking the doctor.
  • Take all the medicines, or clear photos of them, to the follow-up visit.

The first few days: what to watch

Keep a simple notebook: what your parent ate and drank, sleep, pain, toilet, and anything new. It will help the doctor at the follow-up.

Call the doctor if you notice the warning signs they gave you — for example fever, a wound that is redder, more swollen or leaking, much less eating or drinking, or less urine than usual.

Call 108 or 112 straight away for chest pain, sudden breathlessness, fainting, sudden weakness or drooping on one side of the face or body, new confusion, or heavy bleeding.

Preventing falls

Falls are a real risk in the first days home, when someone is weak and on new medicines.

  • Help your parent the first few times they get up. Sit on the edge of the bed for a moment before standing.
  • Keep the walker or stick within reach, and use well-fitting footwear with good grip.
  • Plan night-time toilet trips: a commode, or someone within call.

Preventing bedsores

If your parent spends most of the day in bed:

  • Change their position regularly, as the doctor or nurse advises.
  • Keep skin clean and dry, and sheets smooth.
  • Check the back, hips, heels and elbows every day for redness or broken skin, and tell the doctor or nurse if you see any.

Nurse, caretaker or both?

Look at what the discharge summary asks for at home:

  • Clinical tasks — injections, IV, dressings, catheter or feeding-tube care, monitoring of vitals: these need a registered nurse, working on the doctor’s written instructions.
  • Daily living — bathing, toileting, feeding, help with walking, turning in bed, medicine reminders, company: this is a caretaker’s work.

Many families need both for a while — a nurse for the clinical part and a caretaker for the rest of the day — and then only a caretaker as recovery goes on. Our guide on choosing between a home nurse and a caretaker explains the difference in more detail.

Look after the family member doing the caring

Whoever is doing most of the caring — often a daughter, daughter-in-law or spouse — needs sleep, meals and time away. Share the tasks, take shifts at night, and ask for help early rather than after a week without sleep. Exhausted family members make mistakes, and they fall ill too.

How Movira Care can help

Share the discharge summary with us, along with the medicine list and your doctor’s or hospital’s contact details. We will recommend the right help — a nurse, a caretaker or both — and give you a written quote before you commit.

Our nurses have valid nursing-council registration and work only on your doctor’s written instructions. Our caretakers help with daily living. If a staff member can’t come, we aim to send a replacement within 72 hours.
