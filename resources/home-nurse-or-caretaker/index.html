Home nurse or caretaker: which one does your family need?

What a caretaker does, what only a registered nurse should do, the grey areas in between — and five questions that tell you which kind of help your parent needs at home.

By Movira Care Team26 November 20266 min read

The call usually starts the same way: “We need a nurse for Papa.” Then we ask a few questions, and it turns out Papa needs help getting to the bathroom, eating on time and someone to sit with him in the afternoon. That is real, important care — but it is not nursing.

Other times it is the opposite. A family looks for “someone to stay with Mummy”, and the discharge summary on the table lists daily injections and a dressing that must be changed every two days. That needs a nurse, and getting it wrong can hurt someone.

In Jammu, as in much of India, the word “nurse” is used for anyone who looks after a patient. This guide untangles the two roles so you can ask for the right help, pay for what you actually need, and keep your parent safe.

What a caretaker does

A caretaker — many families say “attendant” — helps with daily living. The work is practical, physical and constant:

  • Bathing, hygiene, changing clothes and toileting, including adult diapers
  • Feeding, and making sure enough water is drunk through the day
  • Helping your parent sit up, stand, walk and move safely from bed to chair
  • Turning a bedridden person regularly and keeping their skin clean and dry
  • Medicine reminders, read from the prescription label — at the right time, with the right meal
  • Company, conversation, a steady routine and an eye on anything that seems different

A good caretaker is the person who notices that your father ate half his usual lunch, or that your mother seems more confused this evening, and tells you. That watchfulness is often what families value most.

What a registered nurse does

A registered nurse has a nursing qualification and a valid registration with a nursing council. A nurse can do everything a caretaker does, and on top of that can carry out clinical tasks on a doctor’s written instruction:

  • Injections, including insulin where the doctor has prescribed it
  • IV lines and drips
  • Catheter care
  • Wound dressing, including surgical wounds and bedsores
  • Feeding-tube care
  • Checking and recording vitals, such as blood pressure, pulse, temperature and oxygen levels, when the doctor has asked for them to be monitored

The important words are on a doctor’s written instruction. A nurse at home works alongside your doctor or hospital, not in place of them.

The grey areas families ask about

Some tasks sit in between, and this is where most confusion happens.

Medicines. A caretaker can remind your parent to take medicines and hand them over as written on the label. A caretaker should not decide doses, crush or split tablets without the doctor’s say-so, or give anything by injection.

Blood pressure and sugar checks. Using a home BP machine or glucometer looks simple, but the reading only helps if it is taken properly and acted on correctly. At Movira Care, a caretaker does these only if they are trained for it and your doctor has asked for it. If the readings decide a dose — for example insulin — that is a nurse’s job.

Bedsores. Preventing them is daily caretaker work: turning, cleanliness, dry sheets and checking the skin. Once there is an open wound, dressing it is a nurse’s job.

Physiotherapy. A caretaker can help your parent do the exercises a physiotherapist has shown, and walk with them safely. Designing the exercises is the physiotherapist’s job, and that is separate from both caretakers and nurses.

Five questions that decide it

  1. Has the doctor prescribed anything to be done at home — injections, dressings, a drip, catheter or tube care? If yes, you need a nurse for those tasks.
  2. Is there a wound, a line or a tube in the body? If yes, a nurse should be involved.
  3. Is the main need help with bathing, toilet, eating and moving? That is a caretaker.
  4. How many hours of help are needed, and is anyone needed at night? This decides the duty — 8 hours, 12 hours, 24 hours or live-in — more than the type of staff.
  5. Will the need change in the coming weeks? After an operation, clinical needs often reduce as the person recovers. The help can change with them.
Rule of thumb: if your doctor has prescribed a clinical procedure at home, you need a nurse. If your parent mainly needs help with daily living, a caretaker is usually right.

Common situations, and what usually fits

Back home after an operation. Read the discharge summary closely. If it lists dressings, injections or a catheter, you need a nurse for that part. Help with walking, bathing and meals is caretaker work. Some families need both for a short while, then only a caretaker as recovery goes on.

After a stroke. Much of the day is about moving safely, eating, toileting and patience — caretaker work. If there is a feeding tube or catheter, a nurse is needed for that care. Your doctor may also advise physiotherapy.

Memory loss or dementia. The need is usually a calm, patient caretaker who keeps a routine, prevents wandering and falls, and does not argue. Clinical tasks are rare unless there are other conditions.

Bedridden at home. A caretaker handles hygiene, turning, feeding and company. A nurse is needed if there are wounds, tubes or a catheter to manage.

A parent living alone while children work outside Jammu. Often what matters most is a reliable caretaker for company, meals, medicine reminders and safety, with someone the family can call for updates.

Why getting it right matters

Safety. An untrained person giving injections or managing a catheter can cause infections and other harm. This is the most important reason to be clear.

Cost. A registered nurse costs more than a caretaker, and rightly so. If your parent mainly needs help with daily living, paying for a nurse means paying for skills that aren’t being used.

The right person for the job. Good caretakers and good nurses are both skilled, in different ways. Matching the role to the need makes it easier for them to do the job well — and to stay.

What neither should ever do

Neither a caretaker nor a nurse should change medicines or doses on their own. That is your doctor’s decision.

Neither replaces emergency care. If your parent has chest pain, sudden breathlessness, fainting, sudden weakness on one side, new confusion or heavy bleeding, call 108 or 112 straight away, then the doctor.

How Movira Care helps you decide

When you call us, we ask about the diagnosis, the medicines, what a normal day looks like and what the doctor has said. If you have a discharge summary or prescription, share it with us. Then we tell you honestly which kind of help fits — even if that means less than you asked for.

Our caretakers help with daily living and do not do clinical tasks. Our nurses have valid nursing-council registration, which we check before they start and write into your agreement, and they work only on your doctor’s written instructions.
